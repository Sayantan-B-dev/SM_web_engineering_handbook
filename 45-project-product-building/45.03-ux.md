### The Five-Step Product Design Process
Product design follows a five-step process rooted in design thinking: **empathise, define, ideate, prototype, and test**. In practice, these steps overlap and loop - you'll return to earlier stages as new insights emerge.

| Step | What happens | Artifacts |
|------|-------------|-----------|
| **Empathise** | Understand users, their goals, frustrations, workflows | User research, personas |
| **Define** | Define the problem to solve | Problem statement, success metrics |
| **Ideate** | Explore solutions | Sketches, concepts |
| **Prototype** | Build testable models | Wireframes, interactive prototypes |
| **Test** | Validate with real users | Usability findings, iteration plan |

### Product Designer vs UX Designer

| Dimension | Product Designer | UX Designer |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Scope** | End-to-end product - from strategy to ship | User experience and usability |
| **Business involvement** | Contributes to roadmap and prioritisation | Focused on user needs and usability |
| **Metrics** | Product KPIs (activation, retention, revenue) | Usability metrics (task success, time-on-task) |
| **Deliverables** | Strategy docs, prototypes, specs, design system tokens | Wireframes, flows, research reports, prototypes |

In short: all product designers do UX design, but not all UX designers own the full product scope. At smaller companies, these roles converge into one person.

### Design Best Practices (2026)
- **Prioritise UX writing** - microcopy is often the most neglected design element.
- **Design for error states** - what happens when things go wrong is as important as the happy path.
- **Observe real user behaviour** - don't rely on opinions.
- **Introduce changes gradually** - radical redesigns disorient users.
- **Use a design system** - consistency compounds over time.

### Deeper Explanation: UX Is The Interface To Your Architecture

User experience decisions constrain engineering: optimistic updates, offline behaviour, and perceived latency are architectural. Three principles cover most of it: **feedback** (every action shows a state within 100 ms), **predictability** (the same action does the same thing, and irreversible actions are marked), and **forgiveness** (undo, drafts, and recoverable errors).

The practical engineering handoff is a state matrix: for every screen, specify loading, empty, error, partial, offline, and permission-denied states. Teams that define these upfront avoid most "unfinished-feeling" bugs and rework.

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Designs that specify only the happy path.** The other states ship undefined and inconsistent.
- **Blocking spinners for >1 s operations.** Use skeletons or progress; users assume failure.
- **No undo for destructive actions.** Confirmations are worse than a five-second undo.
- **Ignoring accessibility in visual specs.** Contrast, focus, and keyboard paths are UX.
- **Latency treated as implementation detail.** Perceived speed is a product decision.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **Design without research** → you're designing for yourself, not users. Talk to 20-30 potential customers first.
- **Skipping error states** → the happy path works, but the failure path crashes. Design both.
- **No design system** → UI becomes inconsistent as the team scales. Invest early.
- **Radical redesigns** → users are disoriented. Introduce changes gradually.
