### The Core Tension
One of the most critical decisions in building any scalable system is choosing between technical debt and shipping new features. In almost every growing system, this tension eventually surfaces. The solution is **not choosing technical debt over features or vice versa**. It is **embedding prioritisation into the development rhythm**. Technical improvements must sit alongside feature delivery during planning, not be treated as secondary.

### The Six-Question Framework (QCon London 2026)
Joy Ebertz, principal engineer at Imprint, presented a practical framework for prioritizing technical debt at QCon London 2026. The core of the presentation was a **six-question framework** designed to help engineering teams evaluate and prioritize technical debt.

| Question | What it asks |
|----------|-------------|
| **1. What is the cost if we don't do anything?** | Breadth and depth of impact across developers and users, including cascading effects like customer churn and developer attrition |
| **2. What is the cost of fixing it?** | Opportunity cost, training engineers on new systems, running dual systems during migration, hidden surprises |
| **3. Do we need to fix it the right way?** | Is an 80% solution at a fraction of the cost sufficient? (e.g., nightly database cleanup scripts, hard-coded limits) |
| **4. Is now the right time?** | Is the system getting worse or better on its own? Will new tools make refactoring easier in the future? |
| **5. Will we be able to get the project to the finish line?** | Cautionary example: a PHP codebase that evolved through multiple framework iterations without ever completing a migration |
| **6. Will a project actually make things better?** | Teams often become so focused on the cons of the current solution that they forget its original pros |

### The Three Categories of Technical Debt

| Category | Description | Example |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| **Intentional** | A known shortcut: the team chose speed over completeness | "We'll hard-code the tax rate for now" |
| **Unintentional** | Quality degrades through small decisions that seem fine individually | Gradual accumulation of inconsistent error handling |
| **Environmental** | The world changes around you | A framework loses community support; a dependency stops receiving patches |

### The "Feature Fallacy"
Many teams, especially in their early days, lean heavily into a "features first" approach. This strategy can be a powerful engine for growth, but it's a gamble - a form of technical debt that, if not managed wisely, can lead to significant problems down the line. The key isn't to avoid this debt entirely, but to **incur it strategically and repay it diligently**.

### The Strangler Fig Approach
For legacy modernization, follow the strangler fig approach: **wrap legacy code in characterization tests, build new components alongside the old system, and route traffic to the new code incrementally**. Two-week cleanup sprints rarely produce lasting results. The debt took years to accumulate, and durable reduction comes from sustained capacity, visible metrics, and incentives that reward codebase health.

### Deeper Explanation: Prioritise By Risk And Leverage

Prioritisation frameworks (RICE, MoSCoW, cost of delay) all reduce to two questions: how much does this change the outcome, and how confident are we? For engineering-heavy work, add leverage: does this make a whole class of future work cheaper (platform, tooling, tests)?

A practical split that works for small teams: a small continuous stream of debt and platform work (10-20% of capacity), a clear owner for the top three product bets, and an explicit "not now" list so the decisions are recorded rather than relitigated.

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Prioritising by who asked loudest.** It rewards escalation over value.
- **Zero investment in leverage.** Every feature costs the same as the last one forever.
- **Estimate-free prioritisation.** Value without cost is incomplete.
- **Too many "in progress" items.** Work in progress is the biggest hidden tax on delivery.
- **No revisit cadence.** Priorities change with new data; schedules should too.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **Treating debt as an engineering cleanup exercise** → organizations fail at managing technical debt because they treat it as an engineering cleanup exercise, rather than addressing the systemic and strategic friction that quietly erodes value.
- **The MVP that became the product** → a product gets built fast to prove market fit. It works. Revenue follows. But instead of reinvesting in a proper architecture once the product proves itself, the company keeps bolting features onto the shortcut version.
- **No debt budget** → the most successful teams dedicate **15-20% of engineering capacity** to debt reduction permanently.
- **Only fixing visible debt** → the most damaging forms of debt do not appear as defects, backlogs or failed builds. They accumulate quietly in architecture, integration seams, data assumptions and operating models.
