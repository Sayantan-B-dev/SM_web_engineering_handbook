### The Core Mental Model
Product building is not "coding a feature." It is a **continuous cycle of learning, building, measuring, and iterating** - where the primary output is not code but **validated learning about what customers will actually pay for**. As the lean startup framework states: the MVP is the primary vehicle for the **Build-Measure-Learn loop**, where teams build minimal products, release them to collect quantitative data on user behavior, and analyze that data to decide whether to change direction or keep going.

In 2026, AI has compressed the distance between discovery and development. Teams can prototype, test and iterate faster, creating more opportunities to develop alongside customers and bring them into working sessions where they can see the product take shape and provide feedback in real time. But this introduces a new temptation: if we can add another feature to an MVP, why wouldn't we? The discipline of saying "no" to features that don't serve the core hypothesis is now more important than ever.

### The 10/90 Problem
Building version one of a tool is often as little as **10-20% of the total lifecycle effort**. The remaining 80-90% is maintenance, security patching, evolving requirements, integration upkeep, and the inevitable moment when the person who built it moves on, and nobody else understands how it works.【44†L24-L25】 Most product building decisions look cheap at v1 and expensive at v3.

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Building for scale before validation.** Infrastructure for users who never arrive.
- **No success metric per feature.** You cannot tell whether it worked.
- **Confusing shipping with learning.** A launch without instrumentation teaches nothing.
- **Deferring quality entirely.** Onboarding, auth, and payments are hard to retrofit.
- **Unbounded MVP.** "MVP" that includes the roadmap is just a v1 without validation.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **Building before validating** → founders spend months and tens of thousands of dollars on products that generate zero revenue.
- **Confusing "minimum" with "low quality"** → your MVP should be polished and reliable within its limited scope. A buggy or poorly designed product will turn users away, even if the core idea is brilliant.
- **Feature creep in MVP** → every feature you add increases complexity, time to market, and the surface area for bugs. Your first version should be so simple that you're almost embarrassed to release it.
