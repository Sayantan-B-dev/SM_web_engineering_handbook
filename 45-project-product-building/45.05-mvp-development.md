### What an MVP Is
A Minimum Viable Product is the **most basic version of a product that provides core value to its early adopters**. It is not a half-baked or buggy version of the final product. It is a functional, stable tool designed to test a **single key business assumption**.

| MVP | Proof of Concept | Prototype |
|-----|-----------------|-----------|
| Tests business viability | Tests technical feasibility | Explores design and usability |
| "Should we build this?" | "Can we build this?" | "Does this feel right?" |
| Functional, stable | May be fragile | No functional backend required |

### The Build-Measure-Learn Loop
In the lean startup framework, building a business is treated as a scientific experiment. The MVP is the primary vehicle for the loop:

```
Build → Turn ideas into minimal products
  ↓
Measure → Release them to collect quantitative data on user behavior
  ↓
Learn → Analyze that data to decide whether to change direction or keep going
  ↓
(repeat)
```

### Feature Prioritization: MoSCoW
The most challenging part of building an MVP is deciding what **not** to build. Use the MoSCoW method:

| Category | Meaning | MVP? |
|----------|---------|------|
| **Must-have** | Core to solving the problem | ✅ Yes |
| **Should-have** | Important but not critical | ❌ No |
| **Could-have** | Nice to have | ❌ No |
| **Won't-have** | Out of scope | ❌ No |

The "Must-haves" are your MVP. Everything else is noise.

### AI's Role in MVP Development (2026)
There is an obvious side effect to making software easier and faster to build: teams can build more. "There's absolutely this drive to add more stuff because we can," says Ajay Singh Thakur, Group Leader at Constellation Dealer Group. "I strongly discourage that." The purpose of AI in the MVP process shouldn't be to squeeze twice as many features into the same development window. It should be to **shorten the time it takes to put something useful in front of a customer**.

For AI-powered features, refinement extends beyond user experience to **accuracy, privacy, security**. If AI allows a team to build an MVP in three months instead of six, the opportunity is to spend the extra time improving the core experience - not adding adjacent features.

### Deeper Explanation: Minimum Viable Means Minimum To Learn

An MVP is the smallest build that produces trustworthy learning about the riskiest assumption. That inverts the usual instinct: instead of cutting the least important 40% of a full feature set, you decide which single question must be answered and build only what answers it.

Useful cuts: use a third-party service instead of building, ship one segment instead of all users, do it manually behind the scenes (concierge), and use a proxy metric if the real one is too slow to move. Protect the parts that are expensive to retrofit: data model, auth, payment flow, accessibility, and anything the user's trust depends on.

### Additional Pitfalls
- **MVP as "v1 with less polish".** It ships the whole surface with none of the quality and validates nothing.
- **No instrumentation.** You cannot tell what happened.
- **Cutting the trust-critical parts.** Auth, payments, and data loss are not MVP-suitable shortcuts.
- **Unclear kill criteria.** Without a threshold and a date, unvalidated features live forever.
- **Manual processes without a plan.** Concierge operations must be automated when volume grows.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **Confusing "minimum" with "low quality"** → your MVP should be polished and reliable within its limited scope. A buggy product turns users away.
- **No defined success criteria** → without defining success and failure criteria in advance, you can't know if your MVP validated anything.
- **Feature creep** → every feature adds complexity, time to market, and bug surface area. Your first version should be so simple that you're almost embarrassed to release it.
- **Building too much because AI makes it easy** → the goal is to learn, not to ship features. AI should compress time-to-customer, not inflate scope.
