### What Microservices Are
Microservices break a product into services where teams can deploy independently, scale specific components without scaling everything, and own discrete domains without coordinating across a shared codebase. At a decent scale and organizational complexity, those properties matter enough to justify the investment.

### The Costs (The "DevOps Tax")
"The costs are equally real. Every service boundary introduces network latency, an additional failure point, and more logs to correlate when something breaks". Each service requires its own CI, deployment, observability, security review, and on-call. Inter-service calls introduce latency, failure modes, and debugging complexity. Data consistency across services becomes a constant design problem.

### The 2026 Backlash
In 2026, the microservices consensus has cracked. High-profile teams have publicly returned to monolithic or modular designs, citing simpler operations, lower cost, and faster delivery. More than **40% of organizations** report regretting at least some of their microservices decisions.

### When Microservices Still Make Sense
Microservices remain the right choice when **independent scaling, team autonomy at scale, or technology diversity is genuinely required**. Large organizations with hundreds of engineers and mature platforms still benefit. So do products with components that have wildly different scaling profiles, like a real-time messaging service alongside a batch analytics pipeline.

### The Five Organizational Signals
Microservices earn their cost only when five specific organizational signals are present: team topology (multiple independent teams), scaling needs (wildly different profiles), data ownership (clear domain boundaries), operational maturity (dedicated platform team), and deployment independence (blocked deploys).

### Essential Patterns
A microservices architecture requires specific patterns to handle distributed system challenges:

| Pattern | Purpose |
|---------|---------|
| **API Gateway** | Single entry point for clients; routing, auth, rate limiting |
| **Service Mesh** | mTLS, service-to-service encryption, workload identity, telemetry |
| **Saga** | Distributed transactions via compensating transactions |
| **CQRS** | Separate read and write models for different scaling needs |
| **Bulkhead** | Isolate resources (connection pools, memory) per service |
| **Anti-Corruption Layer** | Translate between incompatible domain models |
| **Sidecar** | Helper components as separate containers |

The Saga pattern manages data consistency across microservices that have independent data stores. A saga is a sequence of local transactions where each service performs its operation and triggers the next step. If a step fails, the saga runs compensating transactions to undo the preceding changes. This replaces distributed transactions, which are often impractical in a microservices architecture.

---

### Deeper Explanation: You Are Buying Independent Deploys With Distributed Systems Complexity

Microservices pay for independent deployment, independent scaling, and fault isolation with a fixed tax: network partitions, partial failure, distributed transactions, versioned contracts, service discovery, tracing, and much more operational surface. It is worth paying when a specific organisational or scaling pressure exists, and not otherwise.

If you do adopt them, the practices that keep them survivable: **one service per domain with a clear owner**, **contract-first interfaces** (versioned, with consumer-driven tests), **idempotent and retry-safe APIs**, **asynchronous communication for cross-service effects**, **per-service data ownership** (no shared database), **tracing with a correlation id from the client**, and **progressive delivery with canaries per service**.

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Shared database across services.** It couples deployments back together and destroys the main benefit.
- **Synchronous call chains.** Latency and failure multiply: choose async or aggregate at the edge.
- **Distributed transactions.** They are not available; use sagas with compensations and idempotency.
- **No service-level objectives.** Without per-service SLOs you cannot tell which dependency degrades the product.
- **Extracting before measuring.** Extract the component that actually has a different scaling or release profile.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **No platform team** → every service team reinvents CI, observability, and security. Microservices require solid DevOps practices, automated CI/CD, centralized monitoring, and a team that can handle distributed system design.
- **No service mesh** → no mTLS, no workload identity, no consistent telemetry. Service mesh manages communication between workloads and provides workload identity, service-to-service encryption, and traffic policy.
- **Distributed monolith** → services share a database or call each other synchronously in chains. This is the worst of both worlds.
- **No Saga pattern** → distributed transactions are impractical. Use Saga for cross-service consistency.
- **Debugging across six services** → "debugging a distributed trace across six services is a miserable afternoon even with excellent tooling".
- **Microservices for a 10-person team** → you pay the DevOps tax without the benefits. "40% should not have been microservices".
