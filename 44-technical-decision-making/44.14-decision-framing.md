## 44.1 Decision Framing

### The Framing Procedure
Every decision needs a frame before it can be evaluated:

1. **Define the decision** with clear scope boundaries.
2. **Identify decision criteria**: technical (performance, scalability, security), business (cost, time, risk), team (skills, hiring, maintainability).
3. **Weight criteria by stakeholder priority** - distribute 100 points across criteria.
4. **Identify constraints** - non-negotiable requirements that eliminate options.
5. **Define the evaluation method** - scoring scale (1-5), evidence types, confidence levels.

```text
Decision: Which state management library for the dashboard?

Criteria (weighted by stakeholder priority):
- Performance (30 points) - dashboards re-render frequently
- Learning curve (25 points) - team is mid-level
- Ecosystem (20 points) - long-term maintainability
- Bundle size (15 points) - mobile users on slow networks
- TypeScript support (10 points) - type safety is critical

Constraints (non-negotiable):
- Must work with React 19
- Must support server-side rendering
- Must be actively maintained (release in last 6 months)
```

### The Trade-Off Points That Matter
Gartner's 2026 guidance identifies critical dimensions to consider when evaluating architectural decisions:

| Trade-Off Point | Why it matters | Whom to partner with |
|-----------------|---------------|---------------------|
| **Return on Investment (ROI)** | Quantifies expected financial and operational payback | Finance department |
| **Total Cost of Ownership (TCO)** | Includes migration, licensing, maintenance | Finance + Engineering |
| **Net Present Value (NPV)** | Discounts future benefits and costs to today's budget | Finance |
| **Implementation Risk** | Probability that the approach fails | Engineering + Risk |
| **Cybersecurity Risk** | Attack surface introduced by the choice | Security team |
| **Technical Debt** | Long-term maintenance burden | Engineering |

### Deeper Explanation: Frame Before Comparing

A decision is only arguable once it is framed: the question, the constraints, the criteria, the options, and the time horizon. Most disagreements dissolve when the framing surfaces â€” two people arguing about a framework are often optimising different criteria (hiring vs performance vs familiarity).

A usable frame, in six lines:

```text
Question:     Which data-fetching approach do we standardise on?
Constraints:  6-month deadline, 4 engineers, Next.js App Router, no new vendors
Criteria:     onboarding cost, caching correctness, bundle size, migration effort
Options:      Server Components + fetch cache; TanStack Query; SWR
Time horizon: 24 months
Revisit if:   we add a second client or per-request cost grows 5x
```

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Solution-first decisions.** Deciding "use X" before stating the problem.
- **Hidden criteria.** Unstated preferences (familiarity, rÃ©sumÃ© value) dominate the debate.
- **No time horizon.** A 6-month decision and a 5-year decision have different answers.
- **Too many options.** Three or four well-described options beat a list of ten.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **No clear scope** → the decision keeps expanding. Define boundaries before evaluating.
- **Criteria not weighted by stakeholder input** → analyst preference dominates. Distribute points across stakeholders.
- **No constraints** → you evaluate options that can't work. Eliminate them first.
- **Unsubstantiated scores** → every score must be backed by evidence (code, docs, benchmarks, inference, assumption).
