## 44.10 Technical Debt as a Decision

### Subtopic Tree
- Definition: short-term gain at the cost of long-term disadvantage

- Architectural design decisions that incur debt

### What Technical Debt Is
Technical debt arises from decisions that **favour short-term outcomes at the cost of longer-term disadvantages**. Technical debt always implies the notion of time, decisions, and trade-offs.

### When to Deliberately Incur Technical Debt
Deliberately taking on technical debt makes sense when:
- You're experimenting or prototyping.
- Requirements are still evolving or not well established.
- Speed-to-market is critical.
- The "perfect" solution cost is too high.

### Trade-Off Decisions Across Time
Architectural design decisions that incur technical debt often involve choosing between a maintainable and a debt-incurring solution. Engineers tend to prefer the debt-incurring solution if the **level of technical debt in the existing system** is already high.

### Stakeholder Value and Technical Debt
In software projects, the perceived need to maintain **client satisfaction exerts a dominant directional force** on technical debt decisions. This often leads stakeholders to rationalize debt acquisition as acceptable when it satisfies immediate customer needs.

### Deeper Explanation: Debt Is A Lever, Interest Is The Cost

Technical debt is a deliberate trade: ship now with a shortcut, pay interest later (slower changes, more bugs, onboarding friction). Framed that way, the decision has the same structure as any other: what is the benefit now, what is the interest, and when do we repay?

```text
Decision: hard-code the tax table for the initial market instead of building a rules engine
Gain:      ~2 weeks of schedule, one less abstraction to get wrong
Interest:  every new market needs a code change; ~1-2 days each, plus release risk
Repay when: a third market is committed (then build the table-driven engine)
Owner:     checkout team
```

Debt becomes dangerous when it is invisible, unowned, or unmeasured â€” which is why the "repay when" trigger matters more than the initial decision.

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Debt taken silently.** Nobody knows it exists until it is an emergency.
- **No repayment trigger.** It compounds indefinitely.
- **Interest invisible.** Track the symptoms (change failure rate, cycle time, bugs per area).
- **Refactoring without a goal.** Removing debt should target a measurable constraint.
- **Treating all debt as bad.** Some shortcuts are correct trades; document them as such.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **Debt without a repayment plan** → interest compounds. The debt becomes permanent.
- **No quantifiable business value** → debt is incurred on a hunch. Establish measurable value first.
- **Ignoring the existing debt level** → more debt on top of existing debt increases systemic risk.
