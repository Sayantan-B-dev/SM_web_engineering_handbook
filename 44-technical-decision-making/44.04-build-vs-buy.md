## 44.5 Build vs Buy vs Extend

### Subtopic Tree

### The Wrong Frame in 2026
The build vs buy question misleads most teams because it hides the option that fits the most enterprise use cases: **buy and extend**. Pure builds are rare, reserved for regulated workloads, customer-facing differentiators, and deep integrations. Pure buys are almost as rare, since most bought SaaS gets configured, integrated, and extended before it's useful.

### The 10/90 Problem
In enterprise environments, building version one of a tool is often as little as **10-20% of the total lifecycle effort**. The remaining 80-90% is maintenance, security patching, evolving requirements, integration upkeep, and the inevitable moment when the person who built it moves on, and nobody else understands how it works.

### When AI-Assisted Development Changes the Math
AI-assisted development has changed the math for a **narrow set of build cases**. Internal dashboards and integration glue that used to take a quarter can now ship in days with tools like Cursor, Claude Code, and GitHub Copilot. That covers a specific slice of software, not the broad decision.

### The Scoring Model
A practical scoring model assesses risk, cost, and long-term ownership before enthusiasm turns into technical debt:

| Factor | Build | Buy | Buy + Extend |
|--------|-------|-----|-------------|
| **Upfront cost** | High | Low | Medium |
| **Ongoing cost** | High (team + maintenance) | Subscription | Subscription + engineering |
| **Time to value** | Weeks-months | Days | Days-weeks |
| **Differentiation** | High (if core IP) | Low | Medium |
| **Lock-in risk** | None | High | Medium |
| **Maintenance burden** | 80-90% of lifecycle | Vendor-owned | Shared |

### Commodity vs Core Differentiation
The framework in 2026 has shifted from a question of cost to a question of **core differentiation**. Commodity functions (standard operations) should be bought. Systems that process unique IP should be built. Many leaders buy foundational capabilities while building the experience and intelligence layers that differentiate their products.

### Failure Rates
Both paths fail more often than advocates admit. Research from McKinsey and the University of Oxford across more than 5,400 IT projects found that **large IT projects run 45% over budget, deliver 56% less value than predicted, and 17% go so badly that they threaten the company's existence**.

### Deeper Explanation: Buy The Commodity, Build The Differentiator

The question is not cost alone but **strategic importance and maintenance load**. Buy when the capability is commodity, integration is cheap, and vendors are replaceable; build when it is core to the product experience, the market is expensive or unreliable, or the workflow is genuinely novel.

| Factor | Favours buy | Favours build |
|---|---|---|
| Differentiator | Commodity (auth, email, queue) | Core to the product |
| Total cost | Low, predictable | Cheaper at scale |

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Building auth, email, or payments from scratch.** Commodity work with high risk; buy.
- **Buying the core differentiator.** You outsource your advantage and its roadmap.
- **Ignoring integration cost.** The SDK is cheap; the migration, data model, and edge cases are not.
- **Lock-in without an exit plan.** Export formats, standard protocols, and data ownership matter.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **"We can build that in a day"** → yes, but owning the application long term hasn't gotten easier.
- **Building commodity functions** → wasted engineering on non-differentiating work.
- **Buying core IP** → you've outsourced your competitive advantage.
- **No scoring model** → enthusiasm turns into technical debt.
