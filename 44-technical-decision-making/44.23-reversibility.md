### What Reversibility Means
Reversibility in the gradual process of construction of a storage deposit is considered in relation to the decision-making process. An **irreversible decision** is one that irrevocably condemns the exploitation of new information and thus any adjustment in time. A **reversible decision** allows the decision-maker to integrate newly acquired information and revise choices.

### The Tripartite Taxonomy
Actions can be classified as:
- **Fully invertible**: there exists a deterministic inverse.
- **Compensable**: an inverse operation can restore invariants, even if it cannot undo.
- **Irreversible**: no inverse exists.

### Reversibility Decline
Reversibility declines over time: **r(t) = e^(-0.05t)**. The longer you wait to reverse a decision, the harder it becomes. Reversibility depends on monitoring, authority, retained resources, documented versions, communication channels, and a predefined willingness to change course.

### Design Principles for Reversibility
1. **Progressive deployment**: start small, scale gradually.
2. **Modular design**: make the system partially reversible.
3. **Alternative paths**: retain an alternate technology path.
4. **Version control**: record system state to support rollback.

---

### Deeper Explanation: Optimise For Optionality

Reversibility should drive the *process* of a decision, not just its content. Cheap-to-undo decisions should be made fast and locally; expensive ones need written analysis, broad input, and a clear owner. Many teams invert this: heavy process for library choices, casual dismissal of schema and public API decisions.

When a decision is irreversible, use tools that add optionality: behind an interface (encapsulate a vendor), behind a flag (decouple release from deploy), behind data migration tooling (expand/contract), or behind a standards-based protocol (portable formats).

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Heavy process for reversible choices.** Velocity dies.
- **Casual treatment of one-way doors.** Public APIs, schemas, and identifiers are effectively permanent.
- **No encapsulation of vendors.** Replacing a bought service becomes a rewrite.
- **Ignoring the cost of the exit even when reversible.** Reversal has a price; estimate it.
- **Deciding without a date.** Reversible decisions still need a checkpoint to revisit.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **Irreversible decisions without analysis** → no path back. Apply more rigor to Type 1 decisions.
- **No monitoring** → you can't detect the need to reverse.
- **No retained resources** → reversal is too expensive to execute.
- **Waiting too long** → reversibility decays exponentially.
