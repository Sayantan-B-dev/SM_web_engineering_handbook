## 44.3 Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)

### Subtopic Tree
- Definition: lightweight document capturing a significant architectural decision
- Five core components: title, status, context, decision, consequences
- Alternatives considered (the most valuable addition)
- ADR lifecycle: proposed → accepted/rejected → superseded
- Storage: `docs/adr/`, numbered sequentially
- Governance: amend in place vs supersede
- ADR dependencies and dependency graph traversal
- Who writes ADRs (not just architects)

### What an ADR Is
An ADR is a document that describes a choice the team makes about a **significant aspect of the software architecture they're planning to build**. Each ADR describes the architectural decision, its context, and its consequences.

### The Five Core Components
Michael Nygard, who popularised ADRs, suggests they should consist of five things:
1. **Title** - a logical name for the decision.
2. **Status** - adopted, rejected, or superseded.
3. **Context** - the problem or opportunity.
4. **Decision** - what was chosen.
5. **Consequences** - what results from the decision.

### The Most Valuable Addition: Alternatives Considered
One of the most valuable additions is an **alternatives considered section** - a record of the options you evaluated but didn't choose, and why. Without this, future engineers have no way of knowing whether an alternative was already debated and ruled out, or simply never considered. The difference matters enormously when someone shows up three years later, suggesting you switch from REST to GraphQL and the team has no memory of the two-week debate that already happened… three times.

### ADR Lifecycle
```text
proposed → accepted → superseded by ADR-042
                    → deprecated
         → rejected
```

New ADRs start as `proposed`, move to `accepted` or `rejected`. For genuine decision reversals, write a new ADR, mark the old one `Superseded`, and link both ways.

### ADR Dependencies
All new and existing ADRs should include a **Dependencies section** citing upstream ADRs they rely on. This enables dependency graph traversal and impact analysis when an ADR is superseded or deprecated.

### Who Writes ADRs
ADRs shouldn't be the exclusive domain of architects or tech leads. **Any engineer driving a significant decision** should write the ADR.

---

### Deeper Explanation: The Cheapest Institutional Memory

An ADR is a short, immutable document capturing a decision and its context. Its value is not process compliance; it is that a new engineer (or your future self) can learn *why* the system looks like this without re-running the debate. Fifty lines is enough.

```markdown
# 12. Use cursor pagination for list endpoints

## Status
Accepted (2026-10-13)

## Context
Offset pagination skipped and duplicated rows while users paged through
fast-changing lists, and deep offsets forced full scans on large tables.

## Decision
All list endpoints accept `cursor` + `limit` and return `nextCursor`.

## Consequences
+ Stable pages under concurrent writes; index-friendly queries.
- Clients cannot jump to page N; UI needs infinite scroll or "load more".
- The cursor must be opaque and encoded (created_at + id).

## Alternatives considered
Offset pagination (rejected: correctness), keyset with visible ids (rejected: leaky API).
```

### Additional Pitfalls
- **ADRs written after the fact as justification.** Write them at decision time.
- **Overly long documents.** Nobody reads them; keep to one page.
- **Editing old ADRs.** Supersede them with a new record instead of rewriting history.
- **No index.** A directory of ADRs without a README map is hard to navigate.
- **Decisions without consequences listed.** The "why" is the whole point.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **No ADR** → decisions are forgotten, the same debate happens every 6 months.
- **No alternatives considered** → future teams re-propose rejected options.
- **ADR becomes a short novel** → fewer people read it. Keep it digestible - an engineer should read it in 5 minutes.
- **ADR not reviewed annually** → decisions outlive their context. Review within the system they govern.
