### The 2026 Workflow Framework
After studying dozens of thriving open source projects, a four-step workflow has emerged that works in 2026:

1. **Define Contribution Tracks and Roles** - code contributors, documentation writers, triagers, community moderators. Without clear roles, pull requests get ignored because nobody knows who's responsible. A `CONTRIBUTING.md` that spells out exactly how to pick an issue, what a good first issue looks like, and how to become a reviewer.

2. **Set Up a Transparent Backlog** - use an issue tracker visible to everyone, organized by milestones rather than just tags. Every issue should link to design discussion threads. Tagging issues by difficulty and estimated effort lets new contributors filter for "difficulty: starter" and get their first PR within a day.

3. **Automate the Review Pipeline** - integrate CI that runs checks on every PR automatically. Use bots to assign reviewers, flag stalled PRs, and merge trivial fixes after a passing build. Automation doesn't replace human judgment, but it removes administrative overhead so maintainers focus on architecture decisions.

4. **Measure Community Health, Not Just Velocity** - track contributor retention, review latency, and issue resolution time. A project with 100 contributors who each make one commit is less healthy than one with 10 contributors who make 10 commits each.

### Triage Workflow (PyTorch Example)
PyTorch's maintainer guide specifies: **fully triage issues within 1 week of creation**. Ensure PRs assigned to your module are moved through the review process (pre-review, review, or closed while further discussion happens on the issue).

### Burnout Prevention
- **Rotate responsibilities** - no single maintainer should handle everything
- **Set boundaries** - "I review PRs on Tuesdays and Thursdays"
- **Say no** - not every feature request must be accepted
- **Recruit co-maintainers** - the bus factor is real
- **Document everything** - processes should be in the repo, not in one person's head

### Deeper Explanation: Maintenance Is A Queue You Must Bound

Maintaining a project means sustaining a queue of issues, PRs, security reports, releases, and support â€” with limited volunteer hours. Sustainable practice is about bounding that queue: explicit triage days, a "not planned" bucket you are willing to use, automation that handles the mechanical parts (labels, stale, dependabot, release notes), and clear contribution expectations so the work arrives reviewable.

The habits that prevent burnout: publish response expectations rather than promising instant replies, close issues you will not fix with a reason, say no to scope creep early, use bots for triage and CI, delegate with write access once trust is earned, and keep a maintainer's guide so a successor can take over.

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Open-ended promises.** "I'll look at it soon" without a date costs trust and attention.
- **Never closing anything.** A backlog of 2 000 issues means the tracker has no meaning.
- **Doing all reviews yourself.** It caps the project at your free time; delegate deliberately.
- **Releases as a manual ritual.** Automate tagging, changelog, and publishing.
- **No succession plan.** Bus factor one is the most common cause of project death.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **No `CONTRIBUTING.md`** → contributors don't know how to contribute. They leave.
- **No CI** → maintainers manually test every PR. They burn out.
- **No triage SLA** → issues pile up. Contributors feel ignored.
- **Single maintainer** → bus factor of 1. If they leave, the project dies.
- **No funding** → maintainers burn out working full-time for free.
