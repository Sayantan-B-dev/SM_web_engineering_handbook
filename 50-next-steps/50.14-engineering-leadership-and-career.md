### Scope Is The Real Ladder

| Level | Scope | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Junior | a task | needs direction to finish |
| Mid | a feature | finishes features, misses system effects |
| Senior | a system | solves the problem, owns the consequences |
| Staff | multiple systems / the org | optimises locally, misses org dynamics |
| Principal | the company's technical direction | detaches from execution |

The promotion question is never "how good is your code" but "how much ambiguity can you absorb and resolve". Answer the question someone did not know to ask, and you have moved up a level.

### Technical Influence Without Authority

```text
1. Write it down     an RFC or ADR beats a meeting argument
2. Name the trade-off explicitly (cost, risk, reversibility)
3. Prototype the risky part (a spike removes most objections)
4. Seek disagreement early, from the people who must live with it
5. Decide, document, and revisit on evidence - not on who argued longest
```

Influence is earned by being right in public, in writing, repeatedly - and by being gracefully wrong when the evidence changes.

### Communication As A Senior Skill

```text
Bad:  "The CRDT sync layer has a race where remote updates are attributed
       to the local client, causing undo to revert collaborator edits."

Good: "Users occasionally lose their own undo history when a collaborator
       is editing at the same time. Fix is scoped to one module, two days,
       no migration. Risk: touches the undo manager, so it needs the
       convergence test suite green."
```

The second version lets a product manager decide. The first requires them to be an engineer.

### A Concrete 24-Month Plan

```text
Months 0-3    Finish Atlas end to end. Ship it publicly. Write the postmortem-style
              retrospective of every architectural decision and what you got wrong.

Months 3-6    Contribute upstream to two dependencies you used. Land one real fix.
              Write four technical posts from the Atlas experience.

Months 6-12   Lead one cross-team project. Own an SLO. Run an incident as commander.
              Mentor one junior engineer to independent delivery.

Months 12-18  Design a system from scratch for real requirements. Present it for
              review by engineers who did not work on it. Migrate something
              non-trivially without downtime.

Months 18-24  Pick one deep specialism (distributed systems, AI infra, security,
              data). Become the person the team asks. Publish on it.
```

### Deliberate Learning Loop

```text
Build    -> ship something real, with real constraints
Break    -> cause an incident or a regression and fix it properly
Explain  -> write or teach it; gaps become obvious when you must explain
Review   -> read a codebase or design you did not write, and critique it
Repeat   -> with a harder problem each cycle
```

Reading is not learning in this field. Shipping, breaking and explaining is.

### Deeper Explanation: Seniority Is Judgement Under Uncertainty

The technical skills in chapters 01-50 are necessary and not sufficient. What distinguishes a senior engineer is what they do when the requirements are unclear, the data is missing, the system is on fire and everyone is looking at them.

That judgement comes from having made the decisions before and lived with the consequences - which is precisely why the master project and this chapter exist. Read about trade-offs and you will remember them. Choose one, ship it, break it, and fix it, and you will *know* it.

### Additional Pitfalls

- **Optimising for titles over scope.** A promotion without new scope is a title, and it will not last.
- **Silent disagreement.** Agreement in the meeting, objection in Slack, no decision - that is a leadership failure.
- **Mentoring by rewriting.** Taking the keyboard teaches nothing and demoralises the other person.
- **No written record of decisions.** The team relitigates resolved trade-offs every quarter.
- **Learning only new tools.** Frameworks change annually; fundamentals and judgement compound.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **Promoting on coding ability alone** - the new senior cannot lead, and the team loses its best IC.
- **Engineering that cannot explain itself to the business** - budget, headcount and trust go elsewhere.
- **No incident leadership** - chaos and blame during outages, and the same incident recurs.
- **Burnout ignored as a personal problem** - the strongest engineers leave first, taking the context with them.
