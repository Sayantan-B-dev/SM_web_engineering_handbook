### HTTP/3 and QUIC
HTTP/3 (RFC 9114) runs over IETF QUIC (RFC 9000). Default-enabling HTTP/3 in Chrome resulted in improved performance compared not only to HTTP/1 and HTTP/2, but also to Google QUIC.

QUIC uses **UDP** and eliminates TCP's head-of-line blocking. Setting up a secure connection over QUIC takes **one round trip** instead of several.

Several HTTP/3-supported browsers, like Google Chrome, take a strategy to enable backward compatibility for middleboxes blocking or rate-limiting UDP by **racing** a TCP connection with the QUIC one simultaneously.

### Deeper Explanation: The Browser Already Optimises; Stop Fighting It

Browsers ship an enormous amount of automatic networking machinery: connection pools with per-origin limits, HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 multiplexing, DNS and TLS preconnect, speculative prefetch, priority hints, and early hints. The engineer's job is to *not defeat* it. That means:

- **Fewer origins.** Each origin costs DNS, TCP, TLS, and (in HTTP/1.1) a connection slot. Split into CDN domains only when you are deliberately bypassing a cookie-heavy origin â€” and then `preconnect` to it.
- **Right-sized critical path.** HTTP/3 removes head-of-line blocking, so the ordering lever that remains is *server push-like hints*: `preload` for the fonts and hero image, `fetchpriority="high"` for the LCP element, `loading="lazy"` for everything below the fold.
- **Correct caching.** Immutable hashed assets with long TTLs, short-TTL HTML. Without this, every navigation re-downloads what you already had.
- **Compression.** Brotli for text, and don't compress already-compressed formats (images, woff2, wasm).

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Domain sharding as a "best practice".** It costs more than it saves on HTTP/2+.
- **`preload` everything.** Competing preloads delay the actual LCP resource; preload the critical three or four only.
- **Ignoring `fetchpriority`.** The LCP image is often fetched late because it appears late in the markup; mark it instead of reordering your HTML.
- **No `crossorigin` on preloaded fonts.** The preload is discarded and the font is fetched twice.
- **Assuming the network is the bottleneck.** On fast connections, scripting is usually the LCP limiter; check the waterfall before optimising bytes.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **HTTP/1.1 with many small requests** → head-of-line blocking. Upgrade to HTTP/2 or HTTP/3.
- **No `preconnect` to critical origins** → DNS + TCP + TLS adds 200-500ms before the first byte.
- **No `preload` for critical assets** → browser discovers them too late in the waterfall.
- **Caching HTML too long** → stale HTML points to deleted assets. Use short TTL for HTML, immutable for hashed assets.
