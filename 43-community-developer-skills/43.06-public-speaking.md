### The Fear
Public speaking is the most common fear. The counter-intuitive advice: **speak longer**. Lightning talks (3-5 minutes) are often recommended as a good entry point. But if you hate public speaking, speaking for longer - a 15-20 minute talk - gives you more time to settle into the talk and connect with the audience.

### The "Speak Longer" Principle
A 5-minute lightning talk is a sprint. A 20-minute talk is a marathon. In a marathon, you have time to:
- Introduce yourself properly
- Build a narrative arc
- Let the audience absorb each idea
- Recover from a stumble without rushing

### Preparation Techniques
1. **Write the talk first, then the slides.** The slides support the talk, not the other way around.
2. **Practice out loud.** Reading silently is not practice.
3. **Practice with a timer.** Know your actual time.
4. **Record yourself.** You'll catch filler words, pacing issues, and nervous habits.
5. **Practice in front of one person.** Then a small group. Then the actual venue.

### Body Language and Voice
- **Stand still or move deliberately.** Pacing back and forth is distracting.
- **Make eye contact.** Pick three points in the room (left, center, right) and rotate.
- **Speak slowly.** Nervous speakers speed up. Consciously slow down.
- **Pause.** Silence is powerful. Use it to let ideas land.
- **Use your hands.** Gestures make you look more engaged and confident.

### Handling Technical Difficulties
- **Pause.** Don't panic. Take a breath.
- **Acknowledge it briefly.** "We're having a technical issue. Let's give it a moment."
- **Have a backup.** Slides on a USB, demo recorded, code on GitHub.
- **Move on.** If the demo won't work, skip it. The audience won't remember the failure; they'll remember how you handled it.

### Building a Speaking Habit
```
Lightning talk (5 min) → Meetup talk (15 min) → Local conference (30 min) → Regional conference (45 min)
```

Start with lightning talks. Once you're comfortable, extend to 15-minute meetup talks. Then 30-minute conference talks. Speaking is a skill - it improves with practice.

### Deeper Explanation: Manage State, Not Just Content

Public speaking for engineers is mostly state management: nerves, pacing, and recovery. The techniques that work are mechanical rather than confidence-based â€” prepare and rehearse the first 60 seconds until it is automatic, breathe at slide transitions, pause instead of filling silence with "um", and have a recovery line for when you lose your place ("let me come back to the data in a moment").

Content work does most of the heavy lifting: a clear structure means you always know what comes next, and a written one-page outline in hand lets you recover without panic. Experience matters most; small, frequent talks beat one big high-stakes talk.

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Memorising word-for-word.** One stumble derails the whole script.
- **Fighting nerves instead of pacing for them.** Slow down deliberately; it reads as confidence.
- **Ignoring the room's state.** Watch faces and adjust depth.
- **Apologising excessively.** It undermines credibility; fix and continue.
- **No rehearsal on the actual equipment.** Unexpected setup changes timing and comfort.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **No practice** → the talk is a first draft. Practice out loud at least 3 times.
- **Reading from slides** → the audience disengages. Slides are visual aids, not teleprompters.
- **Pacing nervously** → distracting. Stand still or move deliberately.
- **Speaking too fast** → the audience can't follow. Consciously slow down.
- **No backup for demos** → the demo fails, the talk collapses.
