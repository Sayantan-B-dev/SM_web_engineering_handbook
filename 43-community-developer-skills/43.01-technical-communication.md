### Know Your Audience
The first step is understanding what your audience already knows and what they care about. Different stakeholders need different framing:

| Stakeholder | What They Know | What They Care About |
|-------------|---------------|---------------------|
| Product Manager | Product logic, no deep technical | Timeline, scope, user impact |
| CFO / Finance | Budgets, cost, ROI | Cost, risk, business continuity |
| CEO / Founder | High-level business strategy | Revenue impact, competitive risk |
| Marketing Manager | Campaigns, funnels | Speed to ship, customer-facing impact |
| Legal / Compliance | Policy, regulation | Risk, liability, data privacy |

Tailoring how you communicate by audience is **not dumbing it down**. It is professional communication.

### The Single Biggest Shift: Use Outcomes, Not Mechanisms
When you explain a technical concept, start with the outcome the stakeholder cares about, not the mechanism you used to achieve it.

```text
❌ Mechanism-first: "We need to implement a Redis-backed session store
   with TTL-based eviction to reduce database load."

✅ Outcome-first: "We need to make login faster and more reliable.
   The current approach will slow down as more users sign up."
```

### Replace Jargon With Everyday Language
Every technical term is a **translation tax** on your audience. Use analogies to make abstract concepts concrete.

| Technical Term | Everyday Translation |
|---------------|---------------------|
| Indexing | Making lookups faster by organizing data like a book's index |
| Caching | Keeping frequently used data close by so you don't have to fetch it every time |
| Microservices | Breaking a big application into smaller, independent pieces |
| API | A way for two systems to talk to each other |
| Latency | The delay between asking for something and getting it |
| Throughput | How much work the system can handle at once |

### The Three-Layer Explanation
Explain in three layers:
1. **The one-sentence version** - for executives and non-technical stakeholders.
2. **The paragraph version** - for product managers and cross-functional partners.
3. **The technical version** - for engineers and architects.

```text
Layer 1 (one sentence): "We're moving to a new database because the old one is
running out of capacity."

Layer 2 (paragraph): "The old database has a connection limit that we're
approaching. The new one handles more connections and scales horizontally.
This means we can handle 10x the traffic without downtime."

Layer 3 (technical): "PostgreSQL's max_connections is 100. We're at 87.
We're migrating to a connection-pooled setup with PgBouncer in transaction
mode, which multiplexes 1000 client connections over 20 database connections."
```

### The "So What?" Test
After every sentence, ask: "So what?" If your audience can't answer that, you've lost them. Every technical detail must connect to an outcome they care about.

---

### Deeper Explanation: Structure Beats Eloquence

Technical communication works when the reader can act: state the conclusion first (BLUF), then the reasoning, then the detail. That is the opposite of how engineers usually think (chronological, bottom-up), which is why it takes deliberate practice.

The templates worth internalising:

- **Status update**: what changed, what is blocked, what is needed, by when.
- **Design proposal**: problem, constraints, options, recommendation, trade-offs, open questions.
- **Incident summary**: impact, timeline, cause, fix, follow-ups.
- **Bug report**: steps, expected, actual, environment, evidence.

Writing the summary *before* the work is the highest-leverage habit: if you cannot state the goal in two sentences, the task is not yet understood.

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Burying the conclusion.** Readers stop at the first three lines; lead with the answer.
- **Chronological narration.** "First I tried X, then Y" is a debugging log, not a summary.
- **Unlabelled uncertainty.** Say what is known, assumed, and unknown explicitly.
- **Walls of text.** Headings, short paragraphs, and bullets make content reviewable.
- **No ask.** Every update should make the next action obvious.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **Using jargon without translation** → your audience nods but doesn't understand. Decisions are made on incomplete information.
- **Leading with mechanism instead of outcome** → the business head stares at you blankly. You lose the argument.
- **One explanation for all audiences** → the CEO gets bored, the engineer gets frustrated, the PM gets confused.
- **No visuals** → abstract concepts remain abstract. A diagram explains in 5 seconds what 500 words cannot.
