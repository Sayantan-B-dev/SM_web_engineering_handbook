### The AWS New Voices Framework (2026)
The AWS New Voices 2026 program teaches a framework for technical presentations: **Audience → Hook → Message → Visuals → Action**.

**Week 1: Audience First**
A talk is not about the speaker - it is about the audience. Audiences are silently asking three questions:
- Who are you?
- Why should I care?
- How will this help me?

Answering those questions early helps build trust and connection. Introduce yourself with credibility, then immediately pivot to the audience's problem.

**Week 2: Grab Attention**
A great talk begins with a great hook. Options:
- Storytelling
- Bold statements
- Questions
- Statistics
- Visuals
- Quotes

The goal is to create **curiosity, establish credibility, and keep the audience engaged** from the very beginning.

**Week 3: Structure Matters**
A powerful presentation is not about sharing everything you know. It is about delivering **one clear message**. Organize talks with a clear beginning, middle, and end, identifying key takeaways that audiences remember long after the session.

### Slide Design Principles
- **One idea per slide.** If a slide has two ideas, split it.
- **Less text, more visuals.** A slide is a visual aid, not a document.
- **Code slides need to be understandable within 30 seconds** for people unfamiliar with the language.
- **Consistent formatting.** Fonts, colors, layouts should be uniform.
- **High contrast.** Dark text on light backgrounds (or vice versa). Never light gray on white.

### Live Demos
Live demos are the highest-risk, highest-reward part of a presentation.
- **Always have a backup.** Record the demo as a video. If it fails, play the video.
- **Test the demo in the exact environment.** Network, browser, screen resolution, database state.
- **Have a clean state.** Don't demo with production data. Use seed data.
- **Keep it short.** A 3-minute demo that works is better than a 10-minute demo that fails.

### Handling Nervousness
- **Practice out loud.** Reading slides silently is not practice.
- **Practice with a timer.** Know how long your talk actually is.
- **Arrive early.** Test the projector, microphone, and network.
- **Have water.** Dry mouth is real.
- **Focus on the audience, not yourself.** You're there to help them, not to be judged.

### Deeper Explanation: One Idea, Structured For Skeptics

A technical talk is an argument with evidence, not an exposition of everything you know. The structure that survives technical audiences: the problem and why it matters, the constraints, what you tried (including what failed), the decision, the results with numbers, and the risks. One main idea per talk; everything else supports it.

### Additional Pitfalls
- **Covering everything.** A talk that tries to be a book is remembered for nothing.
- **No numbers.** Technical audiences want measurements, not adjectives.
- **Slides used as a teleprompter.** Reading slides loses the room.
- **Ignoring who is in the room.** The framing for executives differs from engineers.
- **No call to action.** End with what you want people to do or decide.

### Real-World Deal-Breakers
- **No clear message** → the audience remembers nothing. One talk, one message.
- **Slides with walls of text** → the audience reads instead of listens.
- **Live demo without backup** → the demo fails, the talk collapses.
- **No practice** → the talk runs 20 minutes over. You're cut off.
- **Reading slides verbatim** → the audience can read. They don't need you to.
- **No hook** → the audience checks their phones in the first 30 seconds.
